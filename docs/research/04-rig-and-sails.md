# Rig and sails

## Short answer

No Dutch masting table for a 74 of 1795–1811 was found online. The rig is built from
three sources, each used for what it is good for:

| What | Source | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the masts stand | The Washington draught — the lower-mast stumps are drawn in the sheer plan | PRIMARY |
| How long every mast and yard is | Steel's 1794 Royal Navy establishment for a 74 | RECONSTRUCTED (British rule on a Dutch ship) |
| How the masts are fitted — tops, caps, crosstrees, pole topgallants | The Rijksmuseum *Vrijheid* model (Amsterdam, c.1782) and the book | SECONDARY / FICTIONAL |

## Mast positions — PRIMARY

Read off the bow-half photograph of NA 4.MST inv. 59 against the drawing's own scale
of feet (0 at the stern, 179 at the bow, about 49.7 px per Amsterdam foot). First
reading, to be refined when the draught is traced:

| Mast | From the after end of the scale | Fraction of 179 ft |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mizzen | about 30 Amst. ft (8.6 m) | 0.17 |
| Main | about 81 Amst. ft (22.9 m) | 0.45 |
| Fore | about 160 Amst. ft (45.4 m) | 0.90 |

The main-mast stump is drawn about 3.4 Amsterdam feet (0.97 m) across at the deck.

## Fittings — from the *Vrijheid* model

Rijksmuseum NG-MC-500, a fully detailed model of the Dutch 74 *Vrijheid* (Amsterdam,
c.1782), masted, re-rigged by restorers in 2002, its yards lying loose beside it.
<https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/NG-MC-500/catalogue-entry>. Seen in the
museum's public-domain photographs (`https://iiif.micr.io/DQApr/…`):

- **Tops** are broad, nearly square with well-rounded corners, planked radially — not
  the British half-moon top.
- **Caps** are plain rectangular blocks.
- **Topmast crosstrees** are light, with no platform.
- **Topgallant masts are poles**: one long spar above the crosstrees, tapering to a
  point, with a small shoulder near its head and no separate royal mast. The book has
  Jack guess that her "pole topgallantmasts" will not bear skysails (ch. 7). The two
  agree. **So: no royal masts, no royals, no skysails.**
- **Bowsprit** with a single jib-boom.
- **Mizzen**: the yards lie loose beside the model, so its gaff or lateen yard cannot be
  read. A gaff spanker and a crossjack yard follow general practice by 1811 —
  RECONSTRUCTED.

## Sail suit

The same four states and names as surprise-model, with the suit a 74 of 1811 carried:

| State | Canvas |
| --- | --- |
| `full` | Fore and main courses; fore, main and mizzen topsails; three topgallants; fore topmast staysail, jib, flying jib (set from the jib-boom end), main staysail, main topmast staysail, mizzen staysail, spanker — the same fifteen sails, by the same names, as surprise-model. Spritsail furled on its yard |
| `topsails` | Three topsails, fore topmast staysail, spanker |
| `storm` | A reefed foresail and a close-reefed main topsail, nothing else — as surprise-model |
| `furled` | Everything furled on its yard |

Not in this version: studdingsails ("aloft and alow" in the book), and the odd
triangles she hangs upside-down from her topgallant yardarms in light airs (ch. 7) —
a good later detail, since nobody else's ship does it.

## Mast positions — Steel's rule as a cross-check

Steel puts a 74's foremast 1/9 of the lower-deck length abaft the stem, the main 5/9
and the mizzen 17/20 (vol. 1 p. 1). Applied to her 179 ft:

| Mast | Steel's rule, from the after end | Read off her draught | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fore | 159.1 ft | about 160 ft | 1 ft |
| Main | 79.6 ft | about 81 ft | 1½ ft |
| Mizzen | 26.9 ft | about 30 ft | 3 ft |

So the Dutch builder placed his masts much as the British rule would. The model uses the
draught's positions. Rake: Steel's *Naval Architecture* folio "Centres of Masts" gives
1 in per yard aft for main and mizzen and ⅛ in per yard for the fore (about 1.6° and
0.2°); the column is taken to be the 74 from its place in the table — probable, not
proven (<https://archive.org/download/elementspractice00stee/page/n525.jpg>).

## Lengths and diameters — Steel's 74, scaled to her

Source: Steel, *Elements and Practice of Rigging and Seamanship* (1794), vol. 1 p. 49,
"Dimensions of Masts and Yards in the Royal Navy", column **"74 GUNS. 1799 Tons."**,
read off the page image:
<https://archive.org/download/bim_eighteenth-century_the-elements-and-practic_steel-david_1794_1/page/n66.jpg>.
Diameters are at the partners (masts) and the slings (yards).

**Scaling.** Steel's own rule (p. 39) makes the main mast half the sum of the lower-deck
length and the extreme breadth: for his 74 (176 ft + 48 ft 8 in) that is 112 ft 4 in,
printed as 111 ft. For the Washington's British survey (168 ft 5 in + 46 ft 2 in) it is
**107 ft 4 in**. Every spar below is therefore scaled by **0.955** from Steel's column,
which keeps his proportions and fits them to her hull. Grade: RECONSTRUCTED.

Not used: the royal yards (no royals), the sprit topsail yard (gone by 1811), the
studdingsail booms and yards (no studdingsails yet), and the 84 ft lateen **mizzen yard**:
in 1794 a 74 still set her driver on a lateen yard ("driver boom" is printed 0-0); by
1811 the gaff and boom had replaced it. The spanker gaff and boom follow surprise-model's
period rule scaled to her — RECONSTRUCTED.

Masthead lengths are not printed; Steel's rule (p. 42) is 5 in per yard of mast for the
main and fore, 4 in for the mizzen and the main and fore topmasts, 3½ in for the mizzen
topmast and the topgallants. Tapers follow his fractional table on the same page, as
surprise-model's `TAPER` already does.

### Sails

Steel's Table IV (vol. 1 p. 149, leaf n244) gives the cloths and depths of every sail of
a 74 — e.g. main topsail 30 cloths at the head, 47 at the foot, 19¾ yd deep. The
generator shapes each sail between its yards, as surprise-model does; the table is used
as a check on the result, not as input.

### Rigging

Steel vol. 2 pp. 32–39 has the standing and running rigging of a 74 (rope sizes and
lengths), not yet extracted; it will be read when the rig is built.
