# The draught she is lofted from

## Short answer

A real Dutch 74 lines plan survives, in Dutch, at very high resolution, free to view:
**the *Washington***, laid down at Amsterdam in 1795 and launched on 9 August 1796.
We use her lines for the Waakzaamheid. **No British fallback is needed.**

| Depth | 22 Amsterdam ft in the hold = 6.23 m | 18 ft 5½ in in the hold = 5.63 m |
| Keel | — | 138 ft 0¾ in = 42.08 m |
| Tons | — | 1,565 20/94 bm |
| Guns | "76 stukken" on the draught | 74: 28 × 32-pdr lower deck, 28 × 18-pdr upper deck, plus quarterdeck and forecastle guns (British fit) |

Sources: the draught; <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Princess_of_Orange_(1799)>.
The two records agree within 1.3 % on length and 0.4 % on breadth once the plank
thickness is allowed for. The depths differ because the two navies measured the hold
to different points.

Her history: captured with the Batavian fleet at the Vlieter on 30 August 1799, taken
into the Royal Navy as HMS *Princess of Orange*, sold for breaking up at Chatham in 1822.

### Units

The Amsterdam foot is **0.283133 m**, divided into **11** Amsterdam inches of
2.574 cm. The title block's "48 9/11" is the proof that this sheet is in Amsterdam
feet of eleven inches.
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_units_of_measurement>. The spec will write
these as `aft(179)`, `aft(48, 9)` beside the British `ft(168, 5)`, as surprise-model
does with `ft()`.

## Why her, for the Waakzaamheid

- She is a **Dutch-built 74 of exactly the right date** (1795–96), designed in the last
  years of the Republic and serving the Batavian navy — the same navy, and the same
  generation of ships, that would still be at sea in 1811.
- O'Brian's Waakzaamheid is "broadsterned", with "bluff bows" (`01-the-book.md`). The
  Washington's body plan shows exactly that: a full, almost flat floor, a round tuck,
  a very full bow.
- Her draught is far better than the one HMS Surprise was built from. Surprise's hull was
  measured off a 6 px-per-foot web scan, with the midship section measured and the
  other stations reconstructed. This sheet gives about **50 px per Amsterdam foot** — eight times finer — and
  every frame is drawn, so every station can be traced rather than reconstructed.

## What the sheet shows (read off the scan)

- **Body plan.** Fore frames numbered 1–10, after frames up to 22, plus diagonals.
  Nearly flat floors, a hard turn of the bilge and strong tumblehome above it. A round
  tuck under a hollow counter.
- **Stern.** Two tiers of glazed stern windows: the lower tier four lights each side of
  the centreline, three panes wide and four deep, between carved pilasters; the upper
  tier three lights a side. A carved frieze between them, and a tall carved taffrail
  with trumpet-blowing figures and garlands. The elevation cannot show depth; the 1797
  dockyard model of this ship at Greenwich (RMG SLR0591) shows the upper tier is an
  **open stern walk** behind a carved balustrade, and **two stern-chase ports** in the
  counter. **No name** on the stern.
- **Quarter galleries.** Two tiers of three glazed lights, raking aft with the stern,
  a carved scroll frieze between the tiers, a carved acanthus crest above and a carved
  pendant drop below. A carved figure stands at the taffrail corner.
- **Head.** Three curved head rails, a full-length standing figurehead — a warrior in a
  plumed helmet, arm thrust forward (the 1797 model shows a Roman warrior with sword and
  raised shield) — trail boards with trophies of arms, two hawse holes either side.
- **Side.** Lower-deck ports just above a deep main wale; a second, narrower wale band
  between the tiers; upper-deck ports above that; channels with chains at the upper
  deck; quarterdeck and forecastle ports in the bulwark; timberheads along the
  forecastle; a double capstan in the waist.
- **Inside.** Decks, orlop, hold bulkheads and pillars, drawn in the sheer.
- **Scale.** A diagonal scale of feet runs the whole length under the keel, from 0 at
  the stern to **179** at the bow — the title block's length — with single feet and
  inches at the after end. Frame stations are numbered above it (1–22 and on). On the
  bow-half photograph it measures 500 px per ten feet.

What it does **not** give, and so must come from elsewhere: the masts and yards (only
the stumps of the lower masts are drawn), the paint, and a marked load waterline (to be
confirmed during tracing).

### A first probe

A quick scan across the full-resolution body plan picks out each frame line as a clean,
separate dark run, with the diagonals and wales between them. The centreline sits about
1,420 px in from the crop edge and the outermost frame 1,250–1,265 px from it — about
25 ft at 50 px per foot, against the 24 ft 5 in half-breadth inside the plank that the
title block gives (the outermost lines will include the wales, which stand proud of
the plank). So the scan is good enough to trace
every station, not only the midship section.

## How it will be traced

1. Pull the full-resolution scans by IIIF region requests. The two photographs overlap
   heavily (the bow-half shot also shows the stern), so each region is read from the
   shot that shows it squarest, and the two are tied together on the keel's scale of feet.
2. Calibrate with the drawing's own scale of feet, and check against the title block
   (179 ft on the lower deck, 48 9/11 ft inside the plank).
3. Trace every station in the body plan semi-automatically (follow each dark line from
   a seed), and the feature lines in the sheer plan: keel, rabbet, stem, sternpost,
   wales, port sills and heads for both tiers, decks at side, rail, sheer.
4. Cross-check the body plan against the waterlines in the half-breadth plan.
5. Verify: integrate the offsets to a displacement and check it against the British
   tonnage and the usual coefficients for a 74.
   Report the block, prismatic and midship coefficients beside surprise-model's.
6. Write `docs/offsets.json` in the same shape surprise-model uses, so the lofting code
   can be reused unchanged.
